### DBQA-1
The Reformation changed the relationship between rulers and the church to a great extent in the Holy Roman Empire, because it moved control over clergy, church property and doctrine from bishops and the pope into the hands of princes and city councils; however, the change was limited, because it created a choice for territories rather than freedom for individuals, and ordinary subjects gained little.

Before 1517 the church was a separate power with its own courts and wealth, and the empire was a patchwork of princes and free cities under a weak emperor, so rulers had long competed with bishops over jurisdiction and revenue. Luther's challenge, spread by the printing press, arrived in this setting. Document 1 shows a Saxon official urging his prince not to surrender Luther despite the Emperor's edict, because townspeople read his pamphlets and might riot; the prince's protection of Luther shows that rulers could use religion to resist imperial and papal authority. Document 4 shows the next step: a Lutheran prince appoints visitors to inspect parishes and suspends the bishop's jurisdiction, acting as an emergency guardian of his subjects' souls. Document 2 shows a city council doing the same, placing convent property under its treasury and refusing to take orders from outside bishops. Together these documents show rulers absorbing church functions. The authors' situations help explain this. The council in Document 2 is a governing body justifying its seizure of wealth as a way to keep the common people calm, so its stress on order serves its own interest, and the prince in Document 4 calls his takeover an emergency measure, which makes a grab for power sound temporary and pious. Document 5, by a Catholic bishop who has lost revenues, complains that princes claim to judge doctrine; as a loser in the change he likely exaggerates when he says the Church held its property for a thousand years and that no one would obey the Emperor, but his complaint confirms that rulers were taking control.

Outside evidence shows how far this went. The Peasants' War of 1524-1525 ended with princes crushing the rebels, and Luther himself urged the princes to put them down, which made Lutheranism depend on rulers. The Schmalkaldic League of Protestant princes later fought Charles V. Document 3 shows the limits: villagers wanted to choose their own pastors and end serfdom, using Scripture to justify it, but these demands did not win. Document 6 shows the settlement of 1555, which gave the choice of religion to the ruler and only allowed unwilling subjects to emigrate, while excluding Calvinists. So the Reformation transformed who controlled the church but not who controlled belief. It strengthened princes against the pope and the emperor while leaving ordinary people with less choice than the peasants had asked for.

Before 1780 most British workers lived in villages and worked at home or in small workshops, often combining wage work with farming, and the enclosure of common land and the growth of the cotton trade had already begun to loosen that rural economy. Then steam power and the factory system rearranged work. Document 3 shows a manufacturing town growing from 25,000 to 303,000 people over eighty years, which indicates a mass movement of workers into towns. The Scientific Revolution changed European views of authority to a considerable extent, because it replaced the authority of ancient texts and church doctrine with the authority of observation, mathematics and experiment in explaining nature; but the change was partial, since religious authority continued to shape politics and many scientists understood their work as serving faith.

In the late Middle Ages, university scholars accepted Aristotle's physics and Ptolemy's earth-centered astronomy as authoritative, and the Catholic Church had woven them into theology. Renaissance humanists had already encouraged scholars to return to original texts and question medieval commentators, which prepared the ground. In 1543 Copernicus proposed a sun-centered system and Vesalius published an anatomy based on dissection that corrected Galen. Later, Galileo used a telescope in 1610 to observe the moons of Jupiter, evidence that contradicted the idea that everything circled the earth, and Kepler used Tycho Brahe's careful observations to show that planets move in ellipses. Francis Bacon argued that knowledge should come from collecting and testing evidence, and Descartes argued that reason, starting from doubt, was the foundation of certainty. These thinkers changed what counted as proof, since a claim now had to be tested by observation or logic rather than supported by a famous name, and the founding of the Royal Society in 1660 gave this method an institution. Newton's Principia of 1687 showed that one law of gravity explained earthly and heavenly motion, which suggested that the universe followed rules discoverable by reason.

However, the challenge to authority had limits. The Church condemned Galileo in 1633 and forced him to recant, which shows that religious authority could still silence ideas, and Newton and Kepler were deeply religious and saw natural laws as evidence of God's design. Moreover, the new science was confined mostly to educated elites, and later Enlightenment thinkers carried its methods into politics. Thus the Scientific Revolution changed how Europeans decided what was true more than it ended the power of the church.

### LEQB-1
The Congress of Vienna maintained stability in Europe to a considerable extent between 1815 and 1850, in the sense of preventing a general war among the great powers, but it did not end the liberal and nationalist pressures it tried to suppress, so its success was real at the level of diplomacy and limited at the level of domestic politics.

The settlement followed twenty-five years of revolutionary and Napoleonic war, and the victors, led by Metternich of Austria and Castlereagh of Britain, wanted to prevent either French domination or renewed revolution. They restored the Bourbons in France, surrounded France with stronger neighbors such as the enlarged Netherlands, and arranged a rough balance of power among Austria, Prussia, Russia, Britain and France. The Quadruple Alliance and later the Concert of Europe gave the powers a habit of consultation, and no war among them broke out until the Crimean War of 1853, which shows the diplomatic settlement worked because it left no power with a strong reason to overturn it. France was treated moderately, which reduced its desire for revenge.

On the other hand, the conservative order could not control nationalism and liberalism. The Carlsbad Decrees of 1819 censored German universities and the press, and Austria intervened to crush revolts in Naples and Piedmont in 1821, but these measures only pushed opposition underground. Britain refused to support intervention, showing that the great powers did not share one view of stability. Greece won independence in the 1820s, Belgium broke away from the Netherlands in 1830, and the revolutions of 1848 shook Austria, France and the German states, forcing Metternich to flee. Thus the Vienna settlement kept great-power peace by balancing states, but its attempt to freeze domestic politics failed, and the stability that lasted was a stability among governments, not among their peoples.
