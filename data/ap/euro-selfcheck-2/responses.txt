### LEQC-1
The French Revolution changed the social structure of France to a considerable extent, because it destroyed the legal privileges that defined the old orders, but it did not remove inequalities of wealth, and under Napoleon a new hierarchy of merit and property took the place of the old hierarchy of birth.

Before 1789 French society was divided into three estates. The clergy and nobility held tax exemptions, feudal dues and the best offices, while the Third Estate paid most taxes. The fiscal crisis of the 1780s and Enlightenment criticism of inherited privilege made this arrangement politically vulnerable. In August 1789 the National Assembly abolished feudal privileges and issued the Declaration of the Rights of Man, which declared men equal before the law. In 1790 noble titles were abolished and the Civil Constitution of the Clergy made priests state employees, while the sale of Church lands moved property to buyers, many of them wealthy bourgeois and some prosperous peasants. These measures ended the legal basis of the estates, so the change in legal status was fundamental.

However, the revolution's social effects had limits. Many nobles kept their estates and wealth, and the Terror, though it killed some nobles, mostly struck other groups and did not erase elite landowners. Poor peasants and urban workers gained little. Women received no political rights, so equality was male and legal. Napoleon confirmed much of the change in the Napoleonic Code of 1804, which established equality before the law and protected property, and careers were opened to talent. But in 1808 he created a new imperial nobility and the Concordat of 1801 restored cooperation with the Church. Thus the revolution replaced a society of birth-based orders with one based on law, property and service to the state, while wealth continued to separate classes. The change was therefore deep in law and uneven in daily life.

### LEQC-4
The French Revolution was one of the most important events in the history of the modern world, and its effects on society were enormous and far reaching. Everywhere in France people felt the influence of the great changes that were taking place, and the old ways of life were challenged by new ideas about how people should live together. Many groups in society were affected in different ways, and some welcomed the changes while others resisted them with great determination. Over the years the country moved through periods of excitement and periods of difficulty, and the people of France faced many struggles. At the same time some older patterns of life continued, because societies do not change overnight. ### LEQC-6
The French Revolution changed the social structure of France only to a limited extent, because the people who held wealth and influence before 1789 largely remained the leaders afterwards, and Napoleon rebuilt a hierarchy that looked much like the old one.

Under the Old Regime the nobility and clergy had tax exemptions and feudal rights, and wealth in land gave real power. The revolution abolished feudal privileges in August 1789 and noble titles in 1790, which changed legal status. But legal change did not equal social change. The sale of Church lands mainly benefited those with money to buy, such as wealthy townspeople, so property moved from one privileged group to another rather than reaching the poor. Many noble families kept large estates, and some returned after the Terror. Women, who had joined the market marches and the revolutionary clubs, gained no political rights, and the Napoleonic Code of 1804 placed wives under their husbands' authority, which shows how limited the equality was. Napoleon also created an imperial nobility in 1808 and relied on notables who owned land, so social leadership went back to a propertied elite. The strongest evidence of real change is that careers became open to talent in the army, but that opportunity was limited by wealth and education. For these reasons I judge the revolution as having changed the social order mainly in law, not in who actually held power.

### DBC-1
Overseas trade and American silver changed the economy of Western Europe to a large extent, by causing inflation, creating new commercial institutions and shifting wealth toward Atlantic states, but the change was uneven and the agrarian base of most economies continued.

After 1492 Spanish and Portuguese voyages opened the Atlantic, and Atlantic ports began to gain weight against Mediterranean trade routes. The discovery of the huge silver mines at Potosi in 1545 sent fleets of silver to Spain. Documents 1 and 2 show the result: a Castilian magistrate says that bread, cloth and rents rose while the wages of labourers did not, and the price index for grain in Castile quadrupled from 1500 to 1600. This was the Price Revolution. The magistrate, as an official anxious about "the order of the towns," emphasizes social danger, so his report is useful for how elites perceived inflation but may exaggerate disorder. Document 6 shows that Spain itself did not keep the benefit, since silver was promised to Genoese creditors, a point supported by the Spanish royal bankruptcies of 1557 and 1596. Thus the new wealth enriched other regions more than Spain.

New institutions also appeared. In Document 3 Dutch merchants ask for one company with open shares so that risk is spread, which led to the Dutch East India Company of 1602, an early joint-stock company; the merchants' purpose was to protect themselves from ruin, which explains why they argued from risk. Document 4 shows governments trying to guide trade through mercantilist policy, although the pamphlet writer was arguing to persuade the king and so overstates how much the treasure of a kingdom depended on trade alone. Document 5 shows a cost: guild weavers were undercut by merchants using cheap rural labour, which reflects the spread of the putting-out system.

However, continuity was strong. Most Europeans still lived by farming, and guilds survived in many towns even as merchants bypassed them. Therefore trade and silver transformed finance and prices and shifted power toward the Atlantic, yet they changed the daily economy of rural Europe only partly.
